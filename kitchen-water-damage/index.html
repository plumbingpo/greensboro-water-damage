Kitchen Water Damage: What Greensboro Homeowners Should Know

A kitchen has more water connections than any other room: the faucet, the drain, the disposal, the dishwasher and the refrigerator line. Most of these connections are tucked into cabinets or hidden at the back of appliances. When one drips, the water runs to the back of the cabinet base and spreads under the floor. Our Greensboro crew locates the leak, dries what it wet, and keeps as much of your kitchen intact as we can.

Cabinets are the expensive part of this problem. Boxes made of particleboard swell quickly and do not shrink back, while plywood and solid wood often dry out well. We pull toe kicks and drill small hidden holes to move dry air under and behind the cabinets, so in many kitchens the cabinets and countertops stay right where they are while everything dries.

Signs to Look For

  • Doors on the cabinets that have gone out of alignment or started sticking
  • The bottom edges of cabinet side panels swelling or flaking apart
  • Wood or laminate flooring that cups or lifts in front of the dishwasher
  • Behind the dishwasher or sink, a wall with staining or blistered paint
  • Along the cabinet line or beside the fridge, vinyl that feels soft or looks stained
  • A damp, stale smell when you open the cabinet under the sink
Our Process

How We Handle Kitchen Water Damage

1

Stop the Source

The fixture or appliance line that leaked is shut off, the refrigerator or dishwasher is pulled if necessary, and we confirm the failed connection so it gets repaired before we start drying.

2

Measure the Wet Area

We check the cabinet bases, the wall at their back and the floor throughout the room with thermal imaging and moisture meters. It is common for water beneath a kitchen floor to spread further than you would guess.

3

Dry in Place

With the toe kicks off, air gets pushed below the cabinets and behind them. An antimicrobial is applied to damp cavities while air movers and dehumidifiers work over several days.

4

Remove Only Damage

We carefully remove any flooring, subfloor or cabinet section that cannot be saved. We confirm dry readings, then reinstall appliances and help you line up any repairs that remain.

Hidden Risks

Risks That Are Easy to Miss

The Ice Maker Line

An ice maker is fed by a thin tube that may lose a few drops each minute. Since the refrigerator is never pulled out, even that much rots the floor and the wall in back of it.

The Wall Behind the Cabinets

The wall at the back of the sink and dishwasher holds drain and supply pipes. Cabinets fully cover that drywall and framing, so a leak can soak them and mold can spread out of sight.

Sink Drain Leaks

Because it leaks just when the sink drains, a loose fitting or a worn disposal seal often goes unnoticed. The water carries food waste, which is why a wet sink cabinet smells and molds so fast.

Behind the Dishwasher

Slowly dripping supply or drain fittings soak the cabinet sides beside the unit and the subfloor below. The appliance hides all of it until the floor changes.

How the Damage Progresses

The first hour

Water Goes Underneath

A failed fitting or hose sends water under appliances and to the rear of the cabinets. Through edges and seams, it works its way under the floor covering.

Hours 24 to 48

Cabinets Begin to Swell

The water is absorbed by particleboard bases and toe kicks, which swell. In the dark, enclosed gap below the cabinets, and at the back of the dishwasher, mold can start.

Day 3 to 7

Flooring Starts to Move

Hardwood cups, laminate edges puff up, and vinyl starts to lift as its adhesive lets go. The doors and drawers on the cabinets go out of alignment.

After a week

Tear Out Likely

There is no restoring cabinet boxes once they have swelled. Mold at the back of the cabinets often forces their removal, countertop included, and the subfloor may be delaminating.
